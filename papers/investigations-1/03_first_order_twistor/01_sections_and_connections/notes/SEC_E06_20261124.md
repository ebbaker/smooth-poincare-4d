## Statement and normalization

Let \(V\) be any oriented Euclidean three-space, representing
\(\Lambda_g^+\) at a point of a smooth oriented positive-definite
Riemannian four-manifold. Let \(W\) be a real symmetric trace-free
endomorphism of \(V\) and write \(|W|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\operatorname{tr}(W^2)\).
In the geometric application \(W=W_g^+\) and ASD means \(W_g^+=0\).
The norm is the curvature-operator Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
[SH-B01][common] and the [first-order manuscript][fot], conventions
preceding `sec:local-sections`.

For the unoriented unit axis \([n]\), choose a unit representative \(n\)
and an orthonormal frame \((u,v,n)\) of positive orientation. The complex
vector \(\xi=(u+iv)/\sqrt{2}\) is null for the complex-bilinear Euclidean
pairing and has Hermitian norm one. Write \(q_W(n)=\langle W\xi,\xi\rangle_{\mathbb C}\)
using the bilinear pairing and the complex-linear extension of \(W\).
Changing \(\xi\) by a unit phase \(e^{i\phi}\) multiplies \(q_W\) by
\(e^{2i\phi}\). Reversing \(n\) exchanges the null line and its conjugate,
which conjugates the evaluation up to unit phase since \(W\) is real.
Thus \(|q_W|\) is a function of the unoriented axis.

For unit representatives define \(d([n],[m])=|n\times m|\). For any
three unoriented axes with \(\delta=\min_{i<j}d([n_i],[n_j])>0\),
\[
 |W|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
 \le \frac{128}{3}\,\delta^{-4}\sum_{i=1}^3 |q_W(n_i)|^2 .
 \tag{1}
\]
The power \(4\) is sharp for the squared-norm estimate. Equivalently,
the loss in the norm estimate is \(\delta^{-2}\), with sharp power \(2\).

## Factorization into two real axes

The zero block satisfies (1) immediately. For \(W\ne 0\), diagonalize in
an orthonormal basis \((e_1,e_2,e_3)\) with
\(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\lambda_3\). Set \(D=\lambda_1-\lambda_3>0\)
and \(c=-3\lambda_2/D\). Trace-freeness gives
\(\lambda_1=(D-\lambda_2)/2\) and \(\lambda_3=(-D-\lambda_2)/2\),
so the ordering gives \(-D\le3\lambda_2\le D\), hence \(|c|\le 1\).
Use the two real unit vectors
\[
 a=\sqrt{\tfrac{1+c}{2}}\,e_1+\sqrt{\tfrac{1-c}{2}}\,e_3,
 \qquad
 b=\sqrt{\tfrac{1+c}{2}}\,e_1-\sqrt{\tfrac{1-c}{2}}\,e_3 .
\]
Here \(a\cdot b=c\). With \(a\odot b=\tfrac12(a\otimes b+b\otimes a)\),
the diagonal entries of \(a\odot b-(c/3)I\) are
\(1/2+c/6,-c/3,-1/2+c/6\). Multiplying by \(D\) recovers the
three eigenvalues of \(W\), and summing their squares gives
\[
 W=D(a\odot b-\tfrac{c}{3}I),
 \qquad
 |W|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=D^2\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac{c^2}{6}\right)
 \le \tfrac23D^2 .
\]
Nullity eliminates the scalar term, and
\(|a\cdot\xi|^2=((a\cdot u)^2+(a\cdot v)^2)/2=|a\times n|^2/2\).
The same calculation for \(b\) gives
\[
 q_W(n)=D(a\cdot\xi)(b\cdot\xi),\qquad
 |q_W(n)|=\frac D2\,d([a],[n])\,d([b],[n]).
 \tag{2}
\]
Each linear factor contributes \(1/\sqrt2\) from the Hermitian-unit
normalization, giving \(1/2\) in the product.

For \(|c|<1\), (2) shows that the two axes of zeros are distinct. For
\(|c|=1\), the axes coincide and (2) reads
\(|q_W(n)|=(D/2)d([a],[n])^2\). To see the projective multiplicities,
choose an oriented orthonormal basis with its third vector \(a\).
The null conic is parametrized by the complex line of
\[
 \xi_0(z,w)=(z^2-w^2,\ i(z^2+w^2),\ 2zw),
 \qquad [z:w]\in\mathbb{CP}^1.
\]
This is a linear change of coordinates of the degree-two Veronese
conic. The factor \(a\cdot\xi_0=2zw\) has two simple zeros, the
conjugate null lines of that axis. The same holds for \(b\) in a basis
adapted to \(b\). For coincident axes the resulting homogeneous
quartic has two antipodal double roots. The pointwise quartic
interpretation agrees with Gover–Hill–Nurowski, *Sharp version of
the Goldberg–Sachs theorem*, Ann. Mat. Pura Appl. 190 (2011),
295–340, Remark 5.2 and Proposition 5.3, pp. 309–310, eqs. (14)–(20).
The spectral calculation above proves the factorization and all
constants directly. [Published text][ghn]

## Projective distance estimate

The sine distance obeys the triangle inequality because
\[
 d([n],[m])=\frac1{\sqrt{2}}\bigl|n\otimes n-m\otimes m\bigr|_{\mathrm{HS}} .
\]
Indeed the squared Hilbert–Schmidt distance between these projectors is
\(2-2(n\cdot m)^2\). An open ball of radius \(\delta/2\) therefore
contains at most one of the three sample axes. The union of the two
such balls about \([a]\) and \([b]\) misses at least one sample \([n_j]\),
including when the balls coincide. Its two distances are at least
\(\delta/2\). By (2), \(|q_W(n_j)|\ge D\delta^2/8\), giving
\(\sum_i|q_W(n_i)|^2\ge D^2\delta^4/64\). Combining this with
\(|W|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\le(2/3)D^2\) proves (1). The constant is sufficient
for all triples. For the orthogonal test triple the preliminary
assessment §4.1 gives the stronger squared-norm constant \(2\).
[Preliminary assessment][prelim]

## Sharpness from one axial family

Fix \(W=e_3\otimes e_3-I/3\), so \(|W|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=2/3\) and
\(a=b=e_3\), \(D=1\). For \(0<t<\pi/4\) use the three samples
\[
 n_j(t)=
 \bigl(\sin t\cos(2\pi j/3),\ \sin t\sin(2\pi j/3),\ \cos t\bigr),
 \qquad j=0,1,2 .
\]
Every pairwise dot product is \(1-\tfrac32\sin^2t\), so
\(\delta(t)^2=3\sin^2t-\tfrac94\sin^4t\). Each sample has distance
\(\sin t\) from the axis of \(W\), and thus
\(\sum_j |q_W(n_j(t))|^2=\tfrac34\sin^4t\) by (2). Consequently
\[
 \frac{\delta(t)^4|W|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2}{\sum_j|q_W(n_j(t))|^2}
 =\frac89\left(3-\tfrac94\sin^2t\right)^2 \longrightarrow 8 .
\]
Since \(\delta(t)\to0\), replacing \(4\) in (1) by any \(p<4\)
forces the required constant to diverge. This family sets a lower
bound of \(8\) on the best universal constant at power \(4\).
